Tijuana & San Diego River watersheds — concept design

A treatment train that lets gravity do the work.

Water enters at the top of the cascade and leaves at the bottom. Nothing is pumped and nothing is dosed: a run of low-head weir pools drops the velocity far enough that solids settle out by density, then hands the water to a mineral bed, then to a planted subsurface cell, then to a final polishing pool before discharge.

Every element in the line is a module with a defined slot — a function, what we specify now, what else can go there, and what has to be true for the swap to hold. The design stays valid as the details change.

02 — Flow logic

What happens at each stage

Read left to right on a wide screen, top to bottom on a phone. The bar runs continuously from inlet to outlet, and the colour changes where the mechanism changes.

  1. ·Inlet

    Diversion

    Flow arrives below grade and is dropped in at the head of the train. The inlet apron dissipates the incoming energy so the first basin starts slow.

  2. 01Pool 1 · Forebay

    Density separation

    Velocity drops below the transport threshold. Grit and coarse silt fall out of the moving water and stay on the floor.

  3. ↓Weir 1

    Grade reset

    A low-head drop resets the hydraulic grade and re-aerates the surface as the flow moves to the mineral zone.

  4. 02Pool 2 · Mineral

    Contact and exchange

    Flow enters across the bed and moves through limestone, then zeolite. pH is buffered, then ammonium and dissolved metals are taken onto exchange sites.

  5. ↓Outlet weir

    Level control

    The raised outlet sets the water level inside the bed and delivers the flow, at a controlled rate, onto the gravel of the planted cell.

  6. 03Pool 3 · Planted cell

    Rhizosphere

    Horizontal flow below the gravel surface, through the root zone. No exposed water, no standing liquid, no sludge surface to build.

  7. ↓Weir 3

    Outlet control

    An outlet control weir sets the level in the planted cell and passes the treated flow to the last basin.

  8. 04Pool 4 · Polishing

    Final settling

    The slowest water in the train. The remaining fine fraction settles onto a limestone floor and the surface returns to ambient.

  9. ↓Outlet

    Discharge

    Free overflow to the receiving channel, below the last drop in the cascade.

03 — Approach

Three decisions that set the whole train

01

Remove mass first, cheaply

Solids are the largest load and the easiest to take out. Drop the velocity and the heavy fraction settles on its own, on a flat floor that equipment can reach. Everything downstream — the media, the root zone, the polishing pool — is bought with that first removal, because none of them should be spending capacity on grit.

02

Then work on what is dissolved

What is left is in solution and needs contact, not settling. The mineral bed does the inorganic phase — buffering pH with limestone, then taking ammonium and metals onto exchange sites in the zeolite. It sits before the planted cell deliberately, so the biological stage receives water it can work on rather than water it has to tolerate.

03

Keep the biology under the gravel

The planted cell runs below its own surface. The water travels through the root zone instead of over it, which keeps the treatment where the roots and the microbial community are, and removes the open liquid surface where a sludge mat would otherwise form. The only thing that leaves the cell is cut biomass — and that is a waste stream with a test and a disposal route, costed in, not sold as a product.

01/Pool 1

Forebay

Velocity drops. Grit and coarse silt settle out by density.

A deep, slow basin that cuts the velocity of the incoming flow far enough that heavy mineral solids stop travelling and drop. It is the only place in the train where the load is deliberately allowed to accumulate, and it is sized to be reached and emptied.

Mechanism

Density separation. Above a threshold shear velocity, grit and coarse silt stay in suspension; below it, the denser fraction settles. The forebay is sized to hold the flow long enough for the heavy fraction to reach the floor, and its floor is a flat, reachable slab so the material can be lifted out rather than left in place.

Swap slot — 01

This slot could be filled three ways or more

Function — what it does in the train

First solids capture. Strips the heavy, fast-settling fraction — grit, sand, coarse silt, road-derived mineral solids — before it can blanket the media downstream.

Current fill — what we specify now

A lined, gravity-fed settling basin with a flat stone floor, a hardened inlet apron for energy dissipation, and a mechanical cleanout access at the downstream end.

Alternates — what else can occupy this slot

  • A two-cell forebay with the first cell drawdown-emptied while the second stays in service.
  • A vortex / swirl separator insert for high-solids flows, keeping the same basin footprint.
  • A coarse trash-and-grit screen rack plus a shallower settling cell, where the incoming load is mostly vegetation and litter rather than mineral solids.
  • A widened stilling reach of channel upstream of the basin, where there is bank width to spare.

Swap constraints — what has to be true

  • Cleanout access must be reachable by the equipment that will actually service it — the basin is only mechanical if the machine can get to the floor.
  • Drawdown capability is a swap constraint: any alternate that cannot be emptied dry cannot be cleaned on a rhythm.
  • If the cleanout rhythm is longer than the fill rate, the basin goes anaerobic and the whole downstream train inherits the problem.
  • A separator insert only works at a defined flow band — it has to be matched to the design flow, not added later.

02/Pool 2

Mineral zone

Water passes through limestone then zeolite. pH buffered, dissolved load taken up.

A subsurface mineral bed. Flow enters across the top of the bed, moves down into the media and travels the length of it in contact with two mineral layers stacked in series. Nothing is dosed and nothing is pumped — the drive is the head between the weirs either side of it.

Mechanism

Contact and exchange. Limestone supplies alkalinity and holds the pore water in a pH band where dissolved metals stay as solids rather than returning to solution. Clinoptilolite is a framework aluminosilicate with an open, charged lattice: it takes ammonium and a share of dissolved metals out of the pore water onto exchange sites as the water moves past. The order matters — the limestone buffer is what keeps the exchange working.

Swap slot — 02

This slot could be filled three ways or more

Function — what it does in the train

Dissolved-phase treatment and pH control, downstream of solids removal and upstream of the biological cell, so the planted cell receives water it can work on.

Current fill — what we specify now

A two-layer subsurface mineral bed: 0.55 m of calcium-carbonate limestone over 0.55 m of clinoptilolite zeolite, on a drainage gravel base with an underdrain, capped with coarse gravel and set between two weirs.

Alternates — what else can occupy this slot

  • Granular activated carbon as a third layer for trace organics, where the incoming flow carries them.
  • Iron-oxide-coated sand in place of clinoptilolite where the target is arsenate rather than ammonium.
  • Biochar from clean vegetative feedstock as a partial substitute for zeolite, blended into the upper layer.
  • A crushed-oyster-shell or recycled-concrete layer as the carbonate source where limestone supply is the constraint.
  • A parallel second bed on a swap-out cycle, so one bed can be taken offline while the other carries the flow.

Swap constraints — what has to be true

  • Media has a finite exchange capacity — the swap constraint is a replacement schedule tied to monitoring, not a lifetime.
  • Media grade and layer thickness set the contact time. Substituting media without re-deriving the layer depth and grade breaks the contact.
  • The carbonate layer has to stay upstream of the exchange layer, or the pH buffer arrives too late to keep metals on the media.
  • Any alternate has to be a material that will not itself leach — a swap is a material specification, not a like-for-like volume.
  • Layering and a gravel cap are what stop the bed short-circuiting. A single graded-fill substitute needs a redesign of the distribution, not a substitution.

03/Pool 3

Planted subsurface cell

Flow moves below the gravel surface, through the root zone. No exposed water.

A long gravel bed planted with native emergent species. The water level sits below the gravel surface and the flow travels horizontally through the root zone, so there is no open water surface, no standing liquid, and no place for a sludge mat to form.

Mechanism

Rhizosphere treatment. Roots leak oxygen into an otherwise reduced gravel matrix, which supports the microbial communities that nitrify and denitrify around them. The gravel itself filters remaining fines and holds a large colonised surface area per unit of volume. Nutrients and metals taken up by the plants are held in the above-ground biomass, which is then cut and removed — the removal is the harvest, not the growth.

Swap slot — 03

This slot could be filled three ways or more

Function — what it does in the train

Biological nutrient and metal uptake, plus fine-solids filtration, at the end of the train where the remaining load is dissolved or near-dissolved.

Current fill — what we specify now

A washed-gravel horizontal subsurface-flow cell with a 0.40 m root zone, planted with native hardstem bulrush, California bulrush, native sedge and broadleaf cattail.

Alternates — what else can occupy this slot

  • Hardstem bulrush (Schoenoplectus acutus) — a species of native sedge, deep-rooted, tolerant of the full flow band. (Native.)
  • California bulrush (Schoenoplectus californicus) — taller, denser stem, more aggressive spread. (Native.)
  • Common spikerush (Eleocharis macrostachya) — lower stature, fills the shallow margins and the inlet apron. (Native.)
  • Rushes (Juncus spp., native, e.g. Juncus effusus) — deep, fine root mass, works in the coarser gravel at the inlet end. (Native.)
  • Saltgrass (Distichlis spicata) — native and salt-tolerant, for cells taking brackish or estuary-influenced flow. (Native.)
  • A mixed-species planting in bands down the cell rather than a single species, which spreads the failure modes across the bed.

Swap constraints — what has to be true

  • Native California species only, or their native alternates. This is a constraint field, not a debate.
  • Planting is by rootstock on set centres, not seed — the bed has to vegetate on the hydraulic schedule, not on a germination season.
  • The water level must stay below the media surface. A species swap that needs ponded surface water is a different cell type, not a swap.
  • Species choice follows salinity: saltgrass holds where estuary-influenced flow reaches the cell; the larger bulrushes do not.
  • Above-ground biomass is contaminated waste. Every species here commits the operator to a harvest cycle, sampling and a disposal route — a real operating cost, not a free resource.
  • Deep-rooted species are the constraint on gravel grade. Too fine a media starves the root zone of oxygen; too coarse will not hold the root mass.

04/Pool 4

Final polishing pool

The last fines settle before discharge.

A shallow, slow, open basin at the bottom of the cascade, edged and floored with limestone riprap. It is the settling buffer between the engineered train and the receiving watercourse, and its depth is set so accumulated material can still be reached.

Mechanism

Final settling and re-aeration. At the bottom of the cascade the flow is at its slowest and the remaining suspended fraction is the fine end of the silt. The run to the outlet gives it residence time for that fraction to reach the floor, and the open surface returns the water to ambient conditions before it re-enters the watercourse. The limestone margin keeps the wetted edge buffered rather than bare.

Swap slot — 04

This slot could be filled three ways or more

Function — what it does in the train

Final solids capture and a stable transition to the receiving channel, plus attenuation of the flow arriving from the planted cell.

Current fill — what we specify now

A shallow open basin with a limestone riprap floor and margin, a vegetated shoreline, and an outlet structure set at the far end.

Alternates — what else can occupy this slot

  • A deeper two-stage basin with a settlable first cell and a shallow vegetated second cell.
  • A riprap-lined stilling reach of channel in place of a basin, where the ground does not allow another cut.
  • A gravel-and-limestone filter berm across the outlet, where the polishing duty is filtration rather than settling.
  • A native marginal planting strip along the wetted edge in place of bare riprap, where shear at the outlet permits it.

Swap constraints — what has to be true

  • Outlet geometry is what sets the upstream water levels. Changing the outlet structure re-levels the whole train, so it is the least swappable element in the line.
  • A basin this shallow only holds if the depth stays within reach of the cleaning equipment — the same rule as the forebay, at the other end.
  • Marginal planting is only an alternate where the shoreline is not the scour zone.
  • Any alternate has to keep the free overflow: a submerged outlet takes level control away from the weirs.

Step 2

Your place

Desert, forest or tundra. This is a constraint layer, not a skin: it decides which plants you are offered, which media can actually be supplied, what the place adds to the water, and how much of its treatment the biology can deliver.

Environment

What this place means for the design

  • Flashy, seasonal flow. Most of the annual volume arrives in a few short events and the train then sits dry. The same footprint gets far less residence time than it would on a steady baseflow, so settling has less time to work.
  • High evaporation. Open pools lose water to the air and concentrate whatever is dissolved in it. Where the flow is exposed, dissolved load and salinity climb rather than fall — evaporative concentration works against the train, and it is why salinity is a design constraint here and not a footnote.
  • Hot summers. Shallow water swings hard in temperature, dissolved oxygen falls as it warms, and the biological stage is least reliable exactly when it is under the most heat and the least shade.
  • Local media. Quarried or produced-here media is carbonate rock, sand, gravel and recycled concrete; exchange and carbon media are hauled-in consumables with a replacement schedule attached.

07 — Built in

Two constraints that are part of the design

Planting is native, and that is fixed

Every planting slot in this train is filled with a native California species or one of its native alternates — bulrush, sedge, cattail, spikerush, rush, and saltgrass where the flow carries estuary-derived salinity. Species that do the same job but do not belong in these watersheds do not appear on this page as options, because they are not options. It is written into the module as a swap constraint so it holds when the details change.

Harvested biomass is waste, and it is costed

The planted cell removes nutrients and metals by growing, which means the removal only actually happens when the growth is cut and taken away. That cut material has been through a treatment cell working on a river that carries urban and agricultural load: it is a contaminated waste stream that needs sampling and a disposal route. Every planting module in this train commits the operator to a harvest cycle, a test, and a place for the material to go. That is an operating cost of the design, not a by-product of it.
