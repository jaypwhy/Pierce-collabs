## 1. IRS FISCAL SPONSORSHIP MODELS — PLAIN LANGUAGE

There is no single IRS "fiscal sponsorship" statute. Practice follows the IRS's framing (commonly via Gregory Colvin / National Network of Fiscal Sponsors taxonomy, Models A–F). The two relevant models:

### Model A — Comprehensive / Direct (the project belongs to the sponsor)
- **Who holds funds:** the sponsor. Donations are made TO the sponsor, earmarked for the project; legally they are the sponsor's money.
- **Who employs:** the sponsor. Project staff are the sponsor's employees (payroll, HR, benefits, workers' comp run through sponsor).
- **Who does the books:** the sponsor. AP/AR, audit, 990 reporting, charitable solicitation registration, insurance.
- **Project's status:** not a separate legal entity; an internal project of the sponsor, governed by a sponsorship agreement + the sponsor's board.
- **Error surface:** NARROWER for the project team — one professional back office, one chart of accounts, one disbursement process, one compliance owner. The residual error risk concentrates in ONE place: the handoff between project instructions → sponsor execution (wrong payee, wrong amount, wrong timing, mis-coded restriction). Mitigation = written disbursement SLAs, dual-approval, and reconciliation (see §3).
- **Tradeoff:** the sponsor's board has ultimate legal control and can freeze or redirect funds if the project goes off-mission. Disclose this to the owner plainly. Fees apply to all revenue flowing through (see §2).

### Model C — Pre-Approved Grant Relationship (the project stays separate)
- **Who holds funds:** the PROJECT (usually an unincorporated association, LLC, or pending nonprofit with its own EIN and bank account). The sponsor makes grants TO the project for specific charitable work.
- **Who employs:** the project. The project runs its own payroll/HR (or a PEO).
- **Who does the books:** the project — with the sponsor exercising oversight: pre-approval of the charitable program, grant reports, expenditure responsibility, right to audit.
- **Project's status:** a separate grantee carrying out its own program with the sponsor's pre-approved grant funds (plus it may hold non-sponsored funds separately).
- **Error surface:** WIDER — two sets of books, two bank accounts, grant-reporting cycles, and every disbursement is a two-step (sponsor→project, project→participant). Each step is a failure point: delayed grant tranches, commingled funds, misreported expenditures, missed reports triggering holds. The project needs its own bookkeeper from day one.
- **Tradeoff:** more autonomy (funds, once granted for the approved purpose, are the project's to execute) and a cleaner glide path to independence — but the project team OWNS its errors instead of inheriting the sponsor's controls.

### Recommendation for Home Key (operator's view, counsel validates)
**Model A for Phase 0–1.** The owner's concern is processing errors; Model A buys a professional back office on day one, minimizes handoffs, and keeps the team focused on participants rather than payroll compliance. Accept the control tradeoff explicitly (sponsor board authority) and negotiate exit/graduation terms in writing up front. Migrate to the independent 501(c)(3) when determination lands — the normal, expected path (verified 2026-09-07: councilofnonprofits.org; pittsburghfoundation.org).

> General IRS mechanism for the grants themselves (verified 2026-09-07): Rev. Rul. 2003-12 — 501(c)(3) grants to distressed individuals from "detached and disinterested generosity" excluded from recipients' gross income under IRC §102. Caveat from prior research: ruling facts are disaster-relief grants; generalizing to ongoing phased grants at our scale is counsel's call.

### Phase map

- **Non-negotiable error controls at every phase:** phone-verified enrollment, batch-test payments, participant confirmation loops, grievance/fix path with SLA, reconciliation cadence. If any lapses, new disbursements pause until restored.
